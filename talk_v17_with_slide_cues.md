> **START ON SLIDE 1 — THE THRESHOLD**

## SCREEN 1 — THE THRESHOLD

The threshold: Everyone says I am crazy, at fifty-five, to come back and “finish” my Ph.D., but *finish* isn't quite the right word for me, and neither is *start over*.

There is some truth in what everyone is saying, though. I am mad. By that I mean I am still a little bit angry, a little bit queer, and a little bit hurt—but not nearly as much as I used to be. Anyway—mad or not—I think the word I am looking for is *liminal*.

I entered graduate school for the first time in 2000. I left a Ph.D. program ABD in 2006 to take care of my family.  And when I came back more than twenty years later, the university still called both versions of me the same thing: *graduate student.* The concept of graduate student conceals both me and graduate student education under layers of problematic performative sentimentality.  

When I came back I had changed. The discipline had changed, of course. Technology had changed. The university had changed. And the academic future I was supposedly moving toward has changed.  How is it that the category of graduate student had not?
I argue that formulation of graduate school and graduate student has long since been problematic and requires our attention.  The performative sentimentality of social, institutional, and disciplinary imaginaries has problematically positioned graduate students as static academic identities stepping through a single threshold. 

And so I ask what can happen if graduate education is not seen as a single threshold leading toward a finished academic self, but a set of moving, unequal thresholds—with little movements, rather just big ones, would that might help us support graduate students without deciding in advance who they must become?

I want to thank my generous chair, co-presenters, and audience members today, as I in this multi-modal presentation play and problematize graduate performance as a poetic state of being both capitol B Being and lower case b being. Thank-you Heidgegger, Luce Irigrary, Homi Babba, and the other many generous mentors I had along the way

And while I talk, if you would like, I invite you to become part of this graduate student performance. Through the site, you can anonymously add a word or short poetic fragment about graduate education. Your words will move through this presentation and eventually into the poem we make together at the end. Think about graduate education–perhaps yours or others, and/or what you know of it–what comes to mind?

THE BIG MOVEMENTS

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 2 — THE ROUTE**

SCREEN 2 — THE ROUTE

Identity, according to Homi Bhabha (1994) is never a final destination. He writes: ‘Identification is never the affirmation of a pre-given identity, never a self-fulfilling prophecy– it is always the production of an image of identity and the transformation of the subject in assuming that image’ (64). Identity is not a matter of what we want to be, or who others want us to be, but rather a contest or struggle between the two. This struggle places each of us in an in-between, unhomely place, somewhere between ‘us’ and ‘them’ and ‘before’ and ‘after,’ a third space.

Graduate education gives us a recognizable route: there’s coursework → the committee then exams and the proposal → then the dissertation → the degree → a job. Milestones, but the route, this route, is not necessarily the poetic journey, that graduate students more likely take.

I want to disrupt the way we think of the route of graduate students, which is popularly as legitimate peripheral participation, the idea that newcomers move from the edges of a community toward fuller participation.  Phil Teeuwsen, Snežana Ratković, and Susan Tilley’s work demonstrates that movement, any movement into a community  is not a neat moment moving from the outside in, and that graduate students are always already moving as newcomers occupying several positions at once–both outsider and insider—student, professional, parent/care giver, cultural community member, depended upon, while also being expendable.  But so is the established and seemingly solid academic, faculty member  is still moving as well and that the categories of *newcomer* and *oldtimer* assume identity can somehow become complete, even though “the journey of becoming an academic, is rarely finished” (684).  

Lindsey Ives and Linnea Spitzer show a similar instability through scholarly writing. Someone can be very experienced as a teacher, dissertation writer, or researcher and encounter a new scholarly genre that returns them to novicehood. They call becoming a scholarly writer “a recursive process where the learner moves non-linearly through stages of liminality from novice to expert” (Ives and Spitzer). Even within the academy an oldtimer can become a new timer again simply by moving into a new genre and or occupying a new position, but these movements are seldom acknowledged.

Not only is the route, not necessarily the journey, but Expertise does not end liminality. A new genre can make you a beginner again. A new method can. A new institution can. A changed discipline can.  And institutional change has become the stable in these insatiable neoliberal times.

Laurie Pinkert and Lauren Marshall Bowen push this farther by questioning the career arc itself. The arc imagines an orderly ascent and makes lives marked by interruption, return, caregiving, contingent work, or movement outside the university look abnormal. They warn that this ascension model can cast real academic lives as “deviant or deficient” (27). Their alternative—disciplinary lifecycling—makes movement, return, overlap, and change part of academic life rather than evidence that someone has fallen off the proper route (16, 27–36).

So many of us might think of graduate school as a bridge between two finished identities, however that is not the case, because the people on both sides of the supposed threshold are unfinished and even the banks on which the bridge is also moving.

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 3 — AN INFRASTRUCTURE OF THRESHOLDS**

## SCREEN 3 — AN INFRASTRUCTURE OF THRESHOLDS

I also want to interrupt the idea that there is only one in-between.  Because there isn't.

Graduate students can be learners and experts, students and workers, teachers and apprentices, insiders and outsiders, necessary and disposable, knowers and people not yet recognized as knowing.

Those positions overlap. And they are not occupied equally.

Beth Godbee keeps us from over romanticizing liminality, particularly the liminality of graduate students. Her work on graduate writers names epistemic injustice as harm done to people in their capacity as knowers. Graduate writers are already working inside asymmetrical relationships with advisors, committees, employers, and disciplinary audiences, and their experiences differ according to how they are historically, socially, institutionally, and culturally positioned (Godbee 2020). And I want to add that they are even sentimentally positioned via social imaginaries, institutional imaginaries, and even discipline imaginaries. 

Further, Godbee defines epistemic rights as rights to “knowledge, experience, or earned expertise” (43–44). She argues that educators and administrators have responsibilities not simply to evaluate graduate writers but to affirm their rights to know, report, claim, relate, and act from knowledge and experience (Godbee_2020). Her work makes an important distinction for me that there is a difference between saying, *I do not know yet* and *being made uncertain about whether you have the right to know*. The first can be generative. It can open inquiry. The second erodes intellectual authority before inquiry has even begun. So if we are going to talk about graduate education as liminal, we need to consider:

Who gets to be unfinished?

Whose uncertainty gets read as intellectual possibility or and as deficiency or incapacity?

And whose uncertainty gets read as evidence that they do not belong?

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 4 — TIME**

## SCREEN 4 — TIME

Another imagined movement I want to disrupt is the notion of how time works for graduate schools and in the institutional state of the academy.  While time may seem like the most visible aspect of graduate students lives, it may actually one of the least visible structures organizing graduate education.

Graduate education appears to be in Chronos time: semesters, funding clocks, deadlines, time-to-degree, publication timelines, job-market cycles, and literally, some institutions track labor through the Kronos system software, a much too perfect image for me to sit aside.

But human development, human being, does not move chronologically in that way. Louise Wetherbee Phelps describes human beings as “dynamic, active systems co-developing over time in relation to equally dynamic systems” and therefore biological, material, social, and cultural running on karotic time (2).

Further, drawing on Jay Lemke, Wetherbee Phelps describes personhood as a “trajectory entity:”someone who cannot be defined at one instant, but only across a life developing through changing relationships with changing environments (14).  Which is how at 55, I find myself back in graduate school as a student to the place where I stopped, but also to no place– no place waiting unchanged for me, which I find disspointing.  Because after all, I was not a person who had been paused for twenty years and neither or younger graduate students, people paused, but we are poetic beings becoming.

Poetry gives us a way to view graduate students journeys as a series of enjambments. Students, some like myself, but also some unlike myself may carry teaching, children, caregiving, illness, money, work, grief, bodies, previous learning, aging, technologies, mistakes, relationships, and entire lives into graduate education. Being “on time” and meeting the milestones of a route tells us very little about nor lets us support the graduate student doing the becoming.

Who is “on time” when lives are not the same? And what does on time mean?  On time for what?  We need to stop teaching graduate students it’s about career arcs any longer, and begin thinking about life cycling

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 5 — THE STATE / A STATE**

## SCREEN 5 — THE STATE / A STATE

The next motion about graduate students I would like to disrupt is: State.

I am not saying that a university is literally a nation-state though the comparison is becoming more and more apt these days with neoliberal university, but I am interested in *state* as a settled condition—as in institutional power that names, classifies, evaluates, credentials, and makes people legible and thus acts and enacts “statehood” the academic institution as state including a state of being:

STUDENT

GTA/ TA / RA / GTI

CANDIDATE

PRODUCTIVE

EXPERT

MARKETABLE

Those categories organize graduate education.  However a problem begins when the category(s) starts becoming the shape the person is supposed to become. Elizabeth Ellsworth's work on pedagogical address suggests that teaching always contains an imagined student. Pedagogy addresses someone before that person has answered back; the student who actually responds can never completely coincide with the learner the institution imagined (Ellsworth 6, 47).

So this is the fifth thing I want us to notice:

Graduate education does not merely move people through an institution. It participates in imagining what an academic is supposed to look like.

And then it can mistake that image for a destination.

What happens when a moving, unfinished person is continually asked to perform a stable academic identity?

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 6 — THE DAEMON**

## SCREEN 6 — THE DAEMON

Around the edges I have been playing with many words, but particularly the word mad.  Stefan Zweig gives me another way to see mad: the daimonic.

He calls it “the unrest that is in us all,” something that presses us outside the familiar (243). Zwieg Stefan Holderlin, Kleist,… And he associates it with the questioning spirit that pushes beyond the ordinary toward what he calls the “dangerous realm of inquiry” (244). As a graduate student, I want the unrest, well at least some of it, not all of it.  For instance, I want:

The question(s) that will not leave.

The connection(s) that appears while reading something apparently unrelated.

The thing someone says in class that sticks with me.

The sentence that arrives before I entirely understand why it belongs.

The feeling that the argument I or someone has written is not quite finished.

And the knowing that I have might also be “right” and to follow that feeling.

But Zweig also warns that the Daemon can “snatch[] the helm” when its tension becomes excessive (244–45).  So I am not suggesting that graduate students obey every impulse, follow every possible lead, or turn exhaustion into evidence of genius, but they should be allowed to follow them more often and graduate student learning needs to be seen in more equal ways and They should be allowed to trust in their own knowing.

Because listening is not obedience, and shouldn’t have to be, and compliance is not the same thing as learning.

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 7 — CONDUCTION / FROM BIG MOVEMENTS TO LITTLE MOVEMENTS**

## SCREEN 7 — CONDUCTION / FROM BIG MOVEMENTS TO LITTLE MOVEMENTS

Writing about Hölderlin, Zweig describes the poet as a “lightning-conductor” who receives an overwhelming force and passes it into form (289–90). But I bring up that image carefully in order to submit it as graduate mentorship: not as a process of reproducing existing scholars, and not as an invitation to chase every impulse wherever it leads, but as a relationship that helps students learn how to conduct intensity into inquiry, craft, evidence, and form.

How do we help students conduct overwhelming intellectual, creative, ethical, and affective energies into forms that can sustain a life of inquiry or even just the moment of graduate school?

Up to this point I have been talking about big movements:

institutional routes,

overlapping thresholds,

unequal recognition,

institutional time,

and the pressure toward a recognizable state.

So, what is to be done?  How can we (re)imagine graduate students and graduate student education? What I have instead are little movements. Just some smaller practices and orientations that might make room for different forms of becoming with/in the institution.

Not remedies.

Not a new universal graduate-school model, for now, though I am sure that is coming.

THE LITTLE MOVEMENTS

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 8 — NOTICE**

## SCREEN 8 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 1: NOTICE

### Intuition + Witnessing

Notice before immediately judging.

Ram Dass calls the intellect an “exquisite tool,” but argues that it should be a servant rather than the master (75). He later describes a movement “from intellect to intuition”—not the abandonment of thinking, but another kind of attention to what is sensed before everything can be conceptually explained (80).

His idea of the witness gives me a practice for that. The witness notices without immediately evaluating. It does not say, *that idea is brilliant* or *that idea is stupid*. It first notices: *that idea keeps returning*.

Maybe we could teach graduate students that too.

*There is that question again.*

*That source keeps bothering me.*

*Something here does not fit.*

*I don't know what this means yet.*

Not everything we notice will become scholarship.

But we cannot examine what we have trained ourselves not to hear.

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 9 — WAIT**

## SCREEN 9 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 2: WAIT

### Active Waiting + Serendipity

Remain open while continuing to do the work.

Maureen Daly Goggin and Peter Goggin describe scholarship as messy and liminal. They write about researchers living along the expanding edge of what they do not know and learning “to stay open to possibilities while they wait actively doing what it is they can do” (4).

They connect serendipity not simply to luck but to Pasteur's “prepared mind”: the reading, thinking, attention, and work that allow somebody to recognize an unexpected possibility when it appears (Goggin and Goggin 4). Goggin Serendipity in Rhetoric

Danielle Koupf makes a similar argument through scrap writing. What feels random can also be inseparable from someone's history, movement through places, prior knowledge, relationships, and deliberate attention. Her students' inventions contain both unpredictability and personal agency (54–55). Where_Chance_and_Invention_Col

Wetherbee Phelps gives this little movement the phrase I may like the best:

“Proceed as way opens” (2). Louise_Weatherbee_phelps_aging_…

Not sit and wait for magic.

Not force the route.

Proceed.

As way opens.  Ram Dass calls this “eating it as is.”

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 10 — ACCOMPANY**

## SCREEN 10 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 3: ACCOMPANY

### Intellectual Companionship + Presence

Do not make graduate students inhabit uncertainty alone, but also do it in a way that does share your own unease and if you can’t do that then at least name and acknowledge your own unease for what it is.

Godbee's response to epistemic injustice is profoundly relational. She writes that “the work of countering epistemic injustice arises in relationship—in the educator affirming and the writer (re)claiming epistemic rights” (44).

Her examples of feminist co-mentoring are not relationships in which one person molds another into a smaller version of herself.

They are forms of power with: listening, affirming, questioning, helping another writer recognize the authority already present in experience and expertise, and then testing what that authority can do in writing (Godbee 45–48).

For me, intellectual companionship means:

*Presence - mine and my faculty.*

*I am here.*

*Show me what you are seeing.*

*You do not have to know all of it yet.*

*What would help us test this?*

That is different from:

*Here is what I did. Become that.*

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 11 — MARK**

SCREEN 11 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 4: MARK

### Little Rituals

Recognize movement before graduation.

Graduate education is very good at the large rituals.

Admission.

Comps.

Defense.

Hooding.

But most becoming happens between those moments.

We need to develop some little rituals–small ways of making movement visible.

The first time a student abandons an argument that no longer works.

The first rejection and resubmission.

Coming back after illness, caregiving, or leave.

Changing a method.

Finishing a chapter that took far longer than anyone expected.

Realizing that the dissertation is no longer the dissertation you thought you were writing.

Pinkert and Bowen's lifecycling and Wetherbee Phelps's trajectory thinking both give us reasons to recognize movement that does not look like linear ascent.

Sometimes the little ritual simply says:

Something changed here.

And that counts. That something. That little. thing.

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 12 — CARE**

## SCREEN 12 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 5: CARE

### Care + Hope

Make intellectual risk materially possible.

Care cannot become one more assignment we hand to graduate students:

*Practice self-care.*

*Set better boundaries.*

*Be resilient.*

Those instructions mean very little if people do not have adequate funding, healthcare, time, flexibility, food, rest, accessible spaces, reasonable workloads, or somebody who will notice when the system has become impossible.

Care has to have material form.

And I am interested in hope here too—not hope as optimism that everything will work out.

Hope as refusing to imagine that the graduate system we currently have is the only graduate system we are capable of making.

Sometimes the little movement is simply asking:

What would make this possible?

instead of:

Why can't you do this the way everyone else does?

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 13 — ALLOW**

## SCREEN 13 — LITTLE MOVEMENT 6: ALLOW

### Unfinishedness

Maybe the smallest movement is to stop treating unfinishedness as deficiency.

Teeuwsen, Ratković, and Tilley tell us that becoming an academic is “rarely finished” (684). Wetherbee Phelps gives us the human being as a trajectory across time rather than a fixed state (14). Pinkert and Bowen replace the career arc with a lifecycle capable of interruption and return.

Taken together, those ideas suggest that the problem may not be unfinished graduate students.

Maybe unfinishedness is part of intellectual life.

A mentor still changes.

A professor encounters a new field and becomes a novice.

A dissertation ends without finishing the question.

A career moves sideways.

Someone leaves.

Someone returns.

Someone becomes something nobody in the program predicted.

The little movement here is not *never finish anything*.

It is:

Do not confuse finishing a requirement with finishing a person.

> **ADVANCE TO SLIDE 15 — BECOMING**

## SCREEN 15 — BECOMING

Graduate education is liminal not simply because graduate students temporarily stand between two stable identities.

The student is moving.

The discipline is moving.

The institution is moving.

The material conditions are moving.

The imagined destination is moving too.

So our work cannot only be getting people successfully across *the* threshold toward a finished academic identity waiting on the other side.

There is no single threshold.

There is no finished academic self.

There may never have been a fixed other side.

Our work with graduate students is also helping people live, write, think, make, question, and become within multiple, unequal, moving conditions—without mistaking unfinishedness for deficiency and without deciding in advance what another person's becoming must look like.

The route may be mapped; The journey cannot be predetermined.

Becoming cannot be predetermined.
